Privacy Policy for WARN

Last updated: 23 October 2026

WARN (Wildlife Alert and Road-hazard Network) is a road-safety app that warns drivers about wildlife and road hazards reported by other drivers nearby. This policy explains what the app collects, what leaves your device, and what it is used for.

No accounts, no sign-in

WARN has no user accounts, no login, no email address, and no password. You are not asked who you are, and the app has no way to know. Nothing in this policy depends on you identifying yourself, because you never do.

How you are identified — you aren't

This is worth stating precisely, because most apps cannot say it. A hazard report submitted by a public user carries no identifier of any kind. What is stored is the hazard's category, its description, its coordinates, the direction of travel, the region, and the time, with the details listed under "Hazard reports" below. There is no account, no user ID, no device ID, and no per-install token attached to a report — not even an anonymous one. The database has no column in which your identity could be recorded, so reports cannot be grouped by who filed them, by us or by anyone else.

The one random value a report does carry is a one-time undo code: it lets you take a report back for about a minute and a half after it reaches our server. A new code is made for every report, it is never stored on your device, and the server keeps only a scrambled (hashed) form of it. Because no two reports share one, it cannot link your reports together.

For a delay report, the same code is used once more. When your device sees you moving freely again — above 15 mph for 45 seconds — it sends one number, how long you were held up, with the code to show it belongs to the same report. No position is sent with it, and your device does not report its position while it waits. If you turn around, leave the road, or clear the report, nothing is sent. Until then the code is held only in the app's memory and is never written to storage; it is discarded when the delay is no longer on the server, when you report another delay, or when the app restarts.

Devices that follow an organisation's notice channel (for example, a cab company's) are different, and deliberately so. Such a device holds a random code for each channel it follows, kept on the device. A code is never shared between channels and never tied to a person. The server keeps only a scrambled form of it, with when the subscription began and when it was last used (updated at most once a day). A subscription that goes unused for 30 days lapses.

Location

Where your device is, moment to moment. WARN needs your location continuously while it is running, because its entire function is to warn you about hazards ahead of you on the road. This runs as a foreground service, so Android shows a persistent notification whenever it is active. This continuous location is used in real time to decide what to alert you about, and is not uploaded to WARN's servers. WARN does keep a road-level log on your device, and it stays there: see "Diagnostic log on your device" below for exactly what it holds and for how long.

Where a hazard is. This is the important exception, and we want to be plain about it: when you report a hazard, the hazard's coordinates are your device's coordinates at that moment, and those coordinates are published — they appear on the map and in the list for every other user in the area. A hazard report is a public statement that something was seen at a particular place at a particular time. It is never attached to your name or to any identifier of you, but it is not private.

Road data stays on your device. WARN ships with the road data for its service area and works out which road you are on by itself. It never asks a server for the roads around you.

Opening the map. When you open the map screen, it fetches map images for the area shown from OpenStreetMap's tile servers, unless an offline map is installed on your device. Those servers therefore learn which area you are looking at, which is usually where you are, along with your IP address, as any website does. This happens only when you open the map; nothing is fetched while it is closed.

Hazard reports

A report consists of the hazard's category, a short description, its coordinates, the direction of travel, and the time. It also records how accurate and how old the location fix was, how the direction of travel was worked out, and which mapped road the report was made on, so a report's position can be judged rather than taken on trust. All of this is readable by other users by design — a hazard nobody else can see would not warn anyone. Reports expire automatically after a period that depends on the hazard type (see "Retention" below).

A delay report — stopped or slow traffic, with no cause named — is stored like any other report, and may later carry one extra number: how long the driver who reported it was held up, which other drivers hear. A delay also has a warning point, which starts at the first report's position. If another driver reports the same delay from a position further back, the warning point moves to that driver's position, and that position is what is published.

WARN does not take or attach photographs, and does not request camera access.

Voice reporting and the microphone

WARN is designed to be used hands-free while driving, so hazards can be reported by voice. This is worth explaining carefully, because it is the part of the app that involves the most outside processing.

  • The microphone is active only while you are making a report — after you press the report control. WARN does not listen continuously, does not use a wake word, and is not recording in the background.
  • Your speech is transcribed to text by your device's speech recognition service, usually Google's, which in normal operation sends the audio to that service's provider for transcription over the network. Some devices can do this offline, but WARN does not rely on that and most devices do not have it available. WARN never sends the audio anywhere itself; the provider's handling of it is governed by that provider's own privacy policy.
  • The resulting text is then sent for classification to Google's Gemini service, reached through a small relay on our own Supabase backend rather than called directly from your phone. This is what lets you say "big moose just past the bridge" in your own words instead of learning a fixed vocabulary. What is sent is the words of your report and the language you use the app in — not your coordinates, not the audio, and no identifier, because there isn't one to send.
  • Google may keep and use the words of your report. WARN uses the free (unpaid) Gemini service. Under Google's terms for it, Google uses the text it receives, and its replies, to provide, improve and develop its products and machine-learning technologies, and human reviewers may read, annotate and process them, after Google has disconnected them from WARN's account and key. So please leave anything personal, such as names or licence plates, out of a voice report. The report buttons and the menu send nothing to Google.
  • WARN does not store the audio. It is not saved on your device and is never uploaded to WARN's own servers. What is kept is the resulting hazard report, exactly as described above.

The same Gemini service is also used to translate the app's own fixed labels and messages into your chosen language. Only that fixed text, and the name of the language to translate it into, is sent: never your reports, your words or anything else about you.

IP addresses

One thing does get recorded, and we would rather name it plainly than leave it out. Because WARN has no accounts, the only way to stop somebody scripting abuse of the classification relay described above is to count requests by network address. When your phone uses that relay, its IP address is stored briefly in a counter table so we can tell whether one source is making an unreasonable number of requests.

That counter holds nothing but the address, a timestamp, and a number. It is not linked to hazard reports — the reports have no identifier to link it to — and rows are discarded shortly after their time window ends. It is not used to identify anyone, to build a profile, or to track a device between sessions.

Our hosting provider's request log. Separately, Supabase, which hosts WARN's server, keeps its own log of every request that reaches it, as web hosts generally do. Each entry records when the request arrived, the network (IP) address it came from, the approximate area of that address (city, postal code, region and country, worked out from the address by Supabase's network provider), the network operator, the name of the software library the app uses to connect (not your phone's model), and which part of the service was asked for: for example, that a report was filed, or that the list of hazards was fetched. It does not record what a report said or where it was.

Because the app fetches the hazard list regularly while it is running, these entries show when a device at a given network address was using WARN, and roughly where that address is. And because the entry for a filed report shows when it arrived, a report could in principle be matched to the network address it came from, for as long as the entry is kept. We are confirming with Supabase how long it keeps these logs. At present, entries from WARN's first day (late July 2026) are still held. We will update this page once we know the exact period. We read this log only to investigate faults and abuse, never to identify or follow anyone.

Diagnostic log on your device

WARN keeps a technical log on the device, so that when something goes wrong the cause can be found rather than guessed at. We want to describe it exactly, because it holds more than its name suggests. It records:

  • Which mapped road you were on, and when. While you drive on mapped roads, the log notes the road's map identifier and your speed, about every 30 seconds, and each time you turn onto another road. Read in order, these entries show the roads you drove and when, for up to 30 days. They do not contain coordinates.
  • The position of each hazard you report, rounded to about 11 metres. Those positions are public anyway, because a hazard report is public by design.
  • Each alert decision: how far away a hazard was, its direction, the direction you were travelling, and whether an alert was shown, spoken or held back.
  • How long the app took to get a GPS fix, how old and how accurate a fix was, and your speed at the moments it records.
  • App events such as starts, stops and crashes.

Alongside the log, the device keeps the text of the last 200 spoken alerts, which can include road names. It also keeps reports you made without a signal, with their exact position, until they are sent. A report the server refuses is kept on the device rather than thrown away. If you turn on the optional bump meter, it keeps measurements of road jolts, with no positions, for up to 30 days.

The log never leaves your device on its own. Nothing uploads it, and WARN's servers never receive it. If we ever ask you to send a copy so that we can investigate a problem you have reported, that is yours to agree to or refuse. If diagnostics are ever collected automatically, this policy will be updated before that happens.

The log holds at most thirty days of activity (and at most 20,000 entries) and trims itself automatically. All of this is stored inside the app's own storage. It is excluded from Android's cloud backup and from device-to-device transfer, so it never leaves the device that way either. Uninstalling WARN deletes it.

Fleet vehicles

WARN can be installed on vehicles operated by a company or agency — for example, taxi or utility fleets — where it may run on a shared device in kiosk mode under a single account belonging to the operator, not to any individual driver. In that configuration the operator's notices reach only devices following its channel. Hazard reports are never restricted this way: every hazard is visible to every user.

Because such devices are shared and carry no personal account, WARN cannot tell which driver is using a fleet device, and does not report driver identity, routes, speed, or behaviour to the operator or to anyone else. WARN is a hazard-warning system, not a driver-monitoring system, and is not designed to be used as one.

Where the data goes

Hazard data is stored on Supabase, which provides the database and backend hosting. Speech transcription and hazard classification involve Google services as described above; on Gemini's free service, Google may also use the text of voice reports for its own purposes, as described under Voice reporting. Map images come from OpenStreetMap's tile servers when you open the map, as described under Location. These providers have their own privacy policies.

Retention

Live hazards. A hazard is removed from the map, and deleted from the live hazard table, when it expires. How long that takes depends on the type:

  • Moose, caribou, bear and other wild animals: 2 hours by day, 6 hours at night, restarting whenever another driver confirms the sighting.
  • Livestock: 3 hours (at most 12). Dogs and poultry: 1 hour (at most 4). People or children in the road: 15 minutes, restarting on confirmation.
  • Weather and visibility (fog, whiteout, smoke, dust, heavy rain, standing water): 2 hours. Snow drifts: 5 hours, or until 6 am if reported at night. Black ice: 2 hours, or until 10 am if reported at night.
  • Crashes: 3 hours; a crash blocking the road: 8 hours; emergency response: 3 hours. Construction: 18 hours. A carcass in the road: 5 hours. Debris: 48 hours. A downed power line: 12 hours. A stalled vehicle: 7 days. A delay with no cause named: 45 minutes, restarting on confirmation (3 hours at most).
  • Potholes and road damage: 180 days. Structural damage: 30 days. Anything else: 2 hours.

Confirmations by other drivers can extend most of these, up to a limit. A hazard a driver clears disappears from the map at once, but is kept out of sight for up to three months before it is deleted.

A delay's held-up time is stored with the delay and deleted with it.

What we keep longer. Three kinds of record outlast the hazard itself:

  • Wildlife sightings archive, kept permanently. When a wildlife report expires, a summary is kept: the animal, its location rounded to roughly 100 by 50 metres, the mapped road, the year, month and hour, how long the report lasted, and how many drivers confirmed it. It holds no exact time, no device information and no link to the original report. It is used to estimate where and when wildlife is a risk. Individual records are not published to app users. For moose, a seasonal summary is published: for areas of roughly 550 by 280 metres, one of three levels showing how much moose activity was reported around this time of year in earlier years, and one to two weeks ago this year. An area is included only if at least two reports contribute to it. The summary holds no dates, no counts and no individual reports. The app receives it for the whole area at once and never sends its own location to ask for it.
  • Recent moose activity, kept for 7 days. For 7 days after a moose report expires, the app can still show that a moose was seen nearby, so the moose button can reflect recent activity. This copy holds only the location rounded to roughly 100 by 50 metres and the hour the moose was last seen. It holds no exact time, no device information and no link to the original report. Unlike the archive above, it is sent to app users, because that is what the button shows. Like a live hazard report, it is published: anyone using the app can read it, and it is not private. The app receives it for the whole area at once and never sends its own location to ask for it. A report that was undone, or removed by an administrator, is never included.
  • Report records, kept with no set end date. For every report, the server keeps a record of its exact position, time and details. These are used to investigate false or disputed hazards. They are readable only by WARN's administrators, never by app users. This includes a report you took back: it disappears from the map straight away, but the server's record of it is kept, marked as withdrawn.

None of these records says who filed a report. There is no user, device, install or IP address column in any of them. Our hosting provider's request logs do record IP addresses; see IP addresses above. What WARN keeps indefinitely is therefore a record of what was seen, where and when, not a record about a person. To be honest about the limit of that: a position and a time are not anonymous in the strongest sense. Someone who already knew where you were at that moment could recognise a report as yours. The records are unidentified, not unidentifiable.

What WARN does not do

  • No advertising, and no sale or sharing of data with advertisers or data brokers.
  • No tracking of individuals, and no profile of any driver.
  • No reporting of police activity or speed traps. WARN is a safety system; this is a deliberate design decision, not an oversight.
  • No collection of contacts, messages, photographs, installed apps, or browsing activity, and no camera access at all.

Your choices

  • Location and microphone permissions can be denied or revoked at any time in your device's settings. WARN cannot warn you about nearby hazards without location, and cannot take voice reports without the microphone, but denying either does not prevent the rest of the app from working.
  • You can request removal of a hazard report by contacting us below. Please include roughly when and where it was reported — since reports carry no identifier, that is the only way we can find the right one.

Children's privacy

WARN is intended for drivers and is not directed at children. It does not knowingly collect information from children.
